Schizophrenia Spectrum

Schizophrenia is more than psychosis. Hallucinations, delusions and disorganized thinking may be part of the picture, but negative symptoms, cognition, functioning, sleep, medication experiences, mood and baseline matter too.

Don't diagnose the moment. Notice what changed, what the person is experiencing, and what your response adds.

HOTSPOT 1 · TV

Same TV. Different meaning.

“That reporter looked at the camera because she was warning me.”

This could fit an idea of reference: ordinary media or events may feel personally directed or specially meaningful. The TV itself has not changed.

Staff lensDo not turn it into a fact-checking contest. Stay grounded in what you observe and ask what the experience means to the person.

Delusions are not one flavor. 🔎

A delusion is a strongly held belief that is not shared by the person's cultural or social context and persists despite contradictory evidence. Staff do not diagnose delusions on the spot; these examples help you recognize patterns clinicians may describe.

Persecutory

“The neighbors installed cameras to watch me.”

The belief centers on threat or persecution. Staff can acknowledge fear and ask about safety without agreeing that surveillance is occurring.

What staff sees ≠ what is happening. 🌫️

Negative and cognitive symptoms are easy to misread because they may look like attitude, laziness or refusal.

Tap the staff label.

What else might be happening?
Reduced motivation / avolition, difficulty initiating, cognitive overload, depression, medication effects, poor sleep, or something else entirely.

Positive ≠ “good.” Negative ≠ “bad.”

Psychotic/positive symptoms add experiences such as hallucinations or delusions.

Negative symptoms involve reductions in motivation, expression, pleasure, speech or social engagement.

Cognitive symptoms can affect attention, working memory, processing and organization.

Key moveDescribe what you observe before assigning intent.

Hallucinations can involve more than hearing voices. 👂👁️✋

Tap a sensory channel. The support question changes depending on the experience, distress and safety impact.

👂

Auditory

Voices, sounds or music may be neutral, comforting, critical, frightening or commanding.

What staff might notice

Pausing as if listening, talking back, covering ears, fear, laughter, distraction or asking whether others heard something.

Ask

“What are you hearing?” “Is it bothering you?” “Is it telling you to do anything?”

Staff lens

Hearing a voice does not automatically mean danger. Understand the experience and follow the person's plan if risk changes.
