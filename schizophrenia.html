🧠 SCHIZOPHRENIA SPECTRUM · EXPLORE, NOTICE, RESPOND

Schizophrenia Spectrum

Schizophrenia is more than psychosis. Perception, meaning, thinking, motivation, expression, sleep, medication experiences and daily functioning can all shift. Explore the person—not a checklist.

A diagnosis is context, not identity. Notice the person first.

Explore the spectrum one layer at a time.

Tap a topic. Try the interaction. Move on when you are ready.

OPTIONAL · AUDITORY HALLUCINATION EXPERIENCE

Keep the simulation playing. Try to do ordinary things at the same time.

This is not a “this is what schizophrenia feels like” exercise. People experience schizophrenia and psychosis differently, and not everyone hears voices. This activity uses externally produced simulations to help you notice what competing auditory experiences can do to attention, memory and communication.

🎧 Headphones recommendedUse a comfortable volume. You control the video and can pause or stop at any time.
↗ You stay in controlSkip the activity if it is not a good fit for you today. Stopping is always okay.
⚠️ Know yourselfIf simulated voices, threatening language or paranoia-themed content could be distressing, skip this experience.
PRESS PLAY. Work through the activities while the simulation plays. Need to pause? Pause it. That is part of what you may notice about the amount of competing input—not a failure.

AFTER THE NOISE

What did you notice while you were trying to function?

Select anything that fit. There is no score and there is no “right” reaction.

Choose what fit your experience.
NOW SWITCH ROLES.

You’re the support person now.

Imagine the person has been trying to manage ordinary tasks while competing experiences were pulling at their attention.

PERSON SUPPORTED
“I DON’T KNOW. STOP ASKING ME.”
They have asked twice what time they need to leave, keep looking toward the hallway, and seem to lose track when you give more than one direction.

THE TAKEAWAY

You listened to a simulation. You did not experience schizophrenia.

The point is not to leave thinking “now I know what it feels like.” The point is to notice how competing input can affect attention, working memory, communication, frustration and trust.
