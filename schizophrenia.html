🧠 PSYCHOSIS · PERCEPTION · THINKING · DIG DEEPER

Schizophrenia + Psychosis

Psychosis can change perception, thinking and the sense of what is real. Schizophrenia can also affect motivation, expression, attention, memory and everyday functioning. The goal is not to out-argue an experience. It is to understand what the person is experiencing, notice what changed, lower unnecessary pressure and support connection, safety and treatment.

Their experience is real to them. Your job is not to make the fear bigger.

First: these words are not interchangeable.

Start with the map. A person can experience psychosis without having schizophrenia, and people with schizophrenia can have very different symptom profiles.

Psychosis

A collection of symptoms involving disrupted thoughts or perceptions and difficulty determining what is real. It can occur with several mental health or medical conditions.

Schizophrenia

A mental illness that can include psychotic symptoms plus negative and cognitive symptoms. Symptoms and functioning vary greatly person to person.

Schizoaffective disorder

Includes psychotic symptoms and significant mood symptoms such as depression or mania. The timing of mood and psychotic symptoms matters clinically.

Paranoia

Suspiciousness or beliefs involving threat, harm or persecution can occur during psychosis. Paranoia describes an experience; it is not the whole person.

About “paranoid schizophrenia”: you may still hear it in older records or everyday language, but DSM-5 removed the old schizophrenia subtypes. It is more useful to describe the person’s current symptoms and support needs than put them into an old subtype box.

Hallucinations are not one thing.

Auditory hallucinations are well known, but hallucinations can involve several senses. The experience, frequency, meaning and distress can be very different from person to person.

Explore the sense involved

Choose one. The important staff question is not just “is this a hallucination?” but “what is the person experiencing, how distressing is it, and is safety affected?”

Auditory

A person may hear voices, sounds, music or other auditory experiences that others do not hear. Voices can be neutral, comforting, critical, frightening or commanding. Do not assume every voice is dangerous.

Staff lensAsk what the voice or sound is like, how the person feels about it and whether it is telling them to do anything unsafe. Stay calm and follow the person's plan and safety procedures when risk increases.

Dig deeper. 🧠

Schizophrenia is much bigger than “hearing voices.” These are the pieces staff often need to understand in real shifts.

👂 Hallucinations

Can involve hearing, seeing, smelling, tasting or feeling things others do not perceive. Ask what the experience is like and whether it is distressing or affecting safety; do not assume every voice is frightening or commanding.

🔎 Delusions + paranoia

Strong beliefs can feel completely certain. Repeated fact-checking, laughing, whispering nearby or trying to “prove” the person wrong may increase mistrust. Stay calm, transparent and grounded in what you observe.

🧵 Disorganized thinking

Thoughts or speech may be difficult to organize. Slow the interaction down. One question at a time, fewer choices, extra processing time and gentle clarification can reduce load.

🌫️ Negative symptoms

Low motivation, reduced expression, withdrawal and difficulty initiating can be mistaken for laziness, defiance or “not caring.” Support initiation without shaming or piling on prompts.

🧠 Cognitive symptoms

Attention, concentration, memory and processing difficulties can affect appointments, routines, conversations and multi-step tasks. Break information down and check understanding without infantilizing.

💊 Medication + side effects

Medication can reduce psychotic symptoms, but response and side effects vary. Staff should notice and document concerns, respect the person's voice, and use the approved medication/refusal and clinical follow-up process rather than giving independent medical advice.

🌱 Recovery

A schizophrenia diagnosis does not erase goals, relationships, work, school, autonomy or identity. Treatment and recovery supports can help people manage symptoms and pursue meaningful lives.

⚠️ Change from baseline

More isolation, less sleep, increasing suspiciousness, self-care changes, confused communication or a sudden functional change may be important. Know the person's usual pattern and follow their individualized plan and clinical/safety procedures when concerns increase.

Keep this in your pocket: “I can believe the fear is real without saying the belief is fact.”
