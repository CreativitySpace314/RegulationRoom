🎧 AUTISM · DIG DEEPER

Autism

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition involving differences in social communication and interaction plus restricted or repetitive patterns, interests or sensory experiences. People can share the diagnosis and still have very different strengths, communication styles and support needs.

Same diagnosis ≠ same person ≠ same support.

Support levels are about support needs.

Autism severity levels describe how much support a person may require in social communication and restricted or repetitive behavior. They are not a score for intelligence, worth, independence or how “autistic” someone is.

Level 1 · requiring support

Support may be needed with social communication, flexibility, organization, transitions or sensory demands even when differences are less obvious to other people.

Level 2 · requiring substantial support

Communication differences, inflexibility, sensory needs or difficulty coping with change may interfere more noticeably across settings.

Level 3 · requiring very substantial support

A person may need extensive support with communication, flexibility, sensory regulation or daily participation. Communication may include speech, AAC, gestures, behavior or a combination.

Support needs can shift. Stress, sensory load, health, communication access, familiarity and the environment can change what someone needs in a particular moment.
Baseline = what is usual for this person. A change from baseline tells you to get curious; it does not tell you the cause.

The spectrum isn't a straight line. 🧠

Think profile, not ranking. Two autistic people can share a diagnosis and have very different patterns of strengths, differences and support needs across areas.

Dig deeper. 🧠

Autism can show up differently across communication, sensory experience, regulation and everyday life. These are some of the pieces worth understanding.

🎧 Sensory processing

Sensory differences can involve sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement, body position and interoception. A person may be sensory-seeking in one area and sensory-avoidant in another. Overload can build quietly before it becomes visible.

Staff lensBefore adding another instruction, look at the environment. Reducing noise, people, light or touch may do more than adding words.

〰️ Stimming

Stimming can include repetitive movement, sound or sensory behavior such as rocking, pacing, hand movements, humming, repeated words, touching textures or moving objects. It may support regulation, expression, focus, enjoyment or sensory needs.

Staff lensDifferent does not automatically mean dangerous. Before trying to stop a stim, ask what it may be doing for the person and whether it is actually causing harm. Changes in usual stimming can also tell you something about stress, excitement or sensory load.

💬 Communication

Communication may include speech, AAC, gestures, scripting, echolalia, facial expression, movement or behavior. Processing what was said and producing a response can take different amounts of time.

Staff lensCommunication ≠ speech only. Ask how this person communicates best, then give enough time for the answer.

🎭 Masking

Masking is camouflaging autistic traits to meet social expectations. Someone may copy other people, suppress movement, force eye contact or rehearse responses. The effort can be exhausting even when the person looked “fine.”

Staff lensLooking regulated does not always mean feeling regulated. Notice what happens after the demand or social setting ends.

🔇 Shutdown

During overload, a person may speak less, move less, withdraw, respond slowly or have difficulty initiating action. That can reflect reduced capacity rather than refusal.

Staff lensMore prompting can become more load. Fewer words, more time and reduced demand may help.

🌋 Meltdown

A meltdown is an overwhelmed nervous-system response. It is not the same thing as a tantrum and should not automatically be interpreted as intentional defiance or a power struggle.

Staff lensThink safety, sensory load and recovery before correction. Teaching can wait until capacity returns.

🔁 Routines + transitions

Predictability can reduce the amount of processing required. Unexpected changes, unclear timing or losing a familiar routine can create distress even when the change seems small to staff.

Staff lensPrepare, don't surprise. Use concrete timing, advance notice and visual information when those supports fit the person.
Autism and intellectual disability are separate.

An autistic person may or may not have an intellectual disability, and a person with an intellectual disability may or may not be autistic. When both are present, both sets of support needs matter without treating either diagnosis as shorthand for the other.
