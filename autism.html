🎧 AUTISM · DIG DEEPER

Autism

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition involving differences in social communication and interaction plus restricted or repetitive patterns, interests or sensory experiences. It is a spectrum because people can have very different strengths and support needs.

Same diagnosis ≠ same person ≠ same support.

About “support levels”

DSM-5 autism severity levels describe the amount of support a person may require in two areas: social communication, and restricted/repetitive behaviors. They are not a score for intelligence, worth, independence or how “autistic” someone is.

Level 1 · requiring support

Differences may be less obvious to others, but social communication, flexibility, organization or transitions can still create meaningful barriers without support.

Level 2 · requiring substantial support

Communication differences, inflexibility, sensory needs or difficulty coping with change may be more apparent and may interfere across settings even with supports in place.

Level 3 · requiring very substantial support

A person may need extensive support with communication, flexibility, sensory regulation or daily participation. Communication may include speech, AAC, gestures, behavior or a combination.

Support needs can look different across domains and situations. Stress, sensory load, communication access, health and familiarity can all change what support someone needs in a particular moment.

And yes: what does “baseline” mean?

Baseline = what is usual for THIS person.

It is the person’s typical way of communicating, moving, sleeping, eating, engaging, regulating, completing routines and responding to support when they are generally doing okay.

So instead of asking, “Is this normal for autism?” staff can ask: “Is this typical for Alex?”

A change from baseline can matter. Someone who usually talks a lot suddenly using very few words, sleeping much more, pacing differently or needing far more support tells you something changed. It does not tell you the cause by itself.

Autism and intellectual disability are separate.

An autistic person may or may not have an intellectual disability. A person with an intellectual disability may or may not be autistic. When both are present, supports should account for both, but neither diagnosis should be used as shorthand for the other.
